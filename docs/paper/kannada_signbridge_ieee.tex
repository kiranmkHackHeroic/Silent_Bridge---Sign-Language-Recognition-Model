\begin{abstract}
Deaf and hard-of-hearing people in Karnataka commonly sign Indian Sign Language (ISL), while the hearing people around them read and speak Kannada, and almost no software connects the two. This paper presents Silent Bridge, a laptop-based system that recognises isolated ISL signs from an ordinary webcam and outputs Kannada text and speech, recognises a closed set of continuous ISL sentences, and in the reverse direction converts Kannada text into an ISL sign sequence played continuously on a 3D avatar. Hand and face landmarks are extracted in the browser with MediaPipe, so no video leaves the user's computer; a motion-based segmenter isolates signs and a bidirectional GRU classifies them. The models are trained only on public ISL datasets (Amrita SLR, INCLUDE and ISL-CSLTR) and are evaluated on signers or recording rounds never seen in training. On 268 held-out recorded videos the isolated-sign accuracy improved from 59.7\% (logistic-regression baseline) to 79.9\% after adding temporal modelling and selectively importing 462 additional INCLUDE recordings; on 213 videos of two completely unseen signers it rose from 54.5\% to 76.5\% (paired McNemar test, $p=0.003$). A fused CTC and sentence-classifier decoder reaches 29.2\% exact-sentence accuracy on 101 corpus sentences. Model updates are deployed only through a promotion gate that compares candidates on identical held-out videos; two candidates were rejected by it. Avatar clips are retargeted from dataset recordings with gap interpolation and smoothing, which removed all mid-sign arm drops, and sentences are signed continuously. We also report robustness, calibration, segmentation, parity and latency analyses and the negative results of the project. Live webcam accuracy has not yet been measured and is identified as the next evaluation step.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sign languages are complete natural languages with their own grammar; they are not signed versions of spoken languages. Indian Sign Language (ISL) is used by a large deaf community, yet automatic recognition research and tools focus mainly on American, German and Chinese sign languages \cite{li2020wlasl,joze2019msasl,koller2015continuous}. For regional languages such as Kannada the gap is wider: a deaf signer in Karnataka and a Kannada-speaking hearing person have no common written or spoken channel without an interpreter, and certified interpreters are few.

A sign is described by several simultaneous parameters: the handshape, the location of the hands relative to the body, the palm orientation, the movement, and non-manual signals such as facial expression and mouth movements. Many signs differ in only one of these parameters, and the same concept can be signed differently in different regions of India. Recognition must therefore capture fine hand detail and its position relative to the face, and must be evaluated on people whose signing the system has never seen.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Given an ordinary laptop webcam and a CPU, the task is (i) to recognise ISL signs performed by a person not present in the training data and present their meaning in Kannada text and speech, (ii) to recognise short continuous ISL sentences, and (iii) to render Kannada text as an ISL sign sequence on an avatar, while reporting honestly how accurate each part is and when the system is uncertain.

\subsection{Objectives and Design Constraints}
The project adopted the following constraints:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{ISL only and public data only}: all training and evaluation data is authentic ISL from published datasets; no other sign language is mixed in, and no signs are invented.
\item \textit{Privacy}: camera frames are processed in the browser; only landmark coordinates reach the server, and nothing is stored unless the user records data deliberately.
\item \textit{Commodity hardware}: training and inference run on a CPU-only laptop.
\item \textit{Honest evaluation}: test sets contain only signers or recording rounds unseen in training; models and thresholds are chosen on development data; negative results are reported.
\item \textit{Safe deployment}: a new model replaces the current one only if it is not worse on the same held-out videos.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item A complete bidirectional pipeline: ISL signs to Kannada text and speech (word level, phrase level and closed-set sentence level), and Kannada text to an ISL gloss sequence rendered on a 3D avatar whose clips are retargeted from real dataset recordings.
\item A landmark-based isolated-sign recogniser with motion segmentation, still-tail trimming and a calibrated rejection threshold, evaluated signer-independently on 268 to 314 held-out videos.
\item A data-centric improvement: selective, checksum-verified download of only the INCLUDE \cite{sridhar2020include} recordings that match the vocabulary (6.0\,GB instead of 44.7\,GB of archives), which raised unseen-signer accuracy by 9.4 percentage points.
\item A continuous-sentence recogniser that fuses a BiGRU-CTC model \cite{graves2006ctc} with a sentence classifier, chosen on a validation round only.
\item A model-governance procedure (identical held-out test sets, paired McNemar tests \cite{mcnemar1947}, a promotion gate and full rollback) that rejected two candidates that looked promising, including a 144-sign model.
\item Avatar clips generated automatically from dataset recordings with tracking-gap interpolation, temporal smoothing and idle trimming, played as continuous signing; this removed all 367 mid-sign arm drops and reduced motion jitter by 36\%.
\item An extensive empirical analysis: calibration, per-sign accuracy, confusions, robustness to perturbations, segmentation variants, browser--Python feature parity, latency and seed variance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Sign Language Datasets}
Large isolated-sign benchmarks such as WLASL \cite{li2020wlasl} and MS-ASL \cite{joze2019msasl} target American Sign Language, and RWTH-PHOENIX-Weather \cite{koller2015continuous} supports continuous German Sign Language recognition and translation \cite{camgoz2018neural}. For ISL, INCLUDE \cite{sridhar2020include} provides 4{,}287 videos of 263 isolated words recorded in Chennai with official train/test splits, ISL-CSLTR \cite{elakkiya2021csltr} provides sentence videos with gloss annotations, and the Amrita SLR dataset \cite{amrita2023} provides isolated signs from several signers. ISLTranslate \cite{joshi2023isltranslate} and iSign \cite{joshi2024isign} offer larger sentence-level ISL corpora aligned with English text but without gloss annotations, which makes them suitable for end-to-end translation but not for gloss-level recognition.

\subsection{Recognition from Video and from Pose}
Appearance-based recognisers learn directly from pixels, for example with 3-D convolutional networks pre-trained on action recognition \cite{carreira2017quo}; they need large datasets and GPUs. Pose-based recognisers first estimate body and hand keypoints, for example with OpenPose \cite{cao2019openpose} or MediaPipe \cite{lugaresi2019mediapipe,zhang2020mediapipehands}, and classify the keypoint sequences. Keypoints reduce the input dimensionality by orders of magnitude and transfer better across backgrounds and clothing. Skeleton-based and pre-trained hand-model approaches \cite{jiang2021skeleton,hu2021signbert} have achieved strong isolated-sign results. With the small per-class data available for ISL, compact recurrent models \cite{cho2014gru,schuster1997bidirectional} on landmark sequences are a practical choice that also runs on a CPU.

\subsection{Continuous Recognition and Translation}
Connectionist temporal classification (CTC) \cite{graves2006ctc} trains a sequence model from a gloss sequence without sign boundaries and is the standard objective for continuous sign language recognition \cite{koller2015continuous}. Neural sign language translation \cite{camgoz2018neural} maps sign videos directly to spoken-language text, but requires large parallel corpora that do not yet exist with gloss annotations for ISL.

\subsection{Signing Avatars}
Avatars can render sign language from notation systems such as HamNoSys \cite{hanke2004hamnosys} or from motion capture. Wolfe \textit{et al.} \cite{wolfe2022avatars} discuss how the representation of signs limits avatar quality and acceptance by deaf users. Our clips are derived from 2-D landmarks of real ISL recordings, which avoids invented motions but limits accuracy; we therefore label every clip as unverified.

\subsection{Positioning of this Work}
Most ISL systems we found report results on random splits that mix a signer's recordings across training and test data, do not output Kannada, and do not provide a reverse text-to-sign direction. This work uses signer- or round-independent splits throughout, provides both directions with Kannada, and documents negative results and the governance used to avoid regressions.

\subsection{Selective INCLUDE Expansion}
INCLUDE is distributed as category archives totalling 56.8\,GB, while only a fraction of its classes match the vocabulary. Because a ZIP archive stores its central directory at the end of the file, we read the directory with HTTP range requests and download only the member videos whose class matches the vocabulary, verifying each against the CRC-32 stored in the archive (Algorithm~\ref{alg:download}). Classes are matched by name against the official metadata. Table~\ref{tab:download} summarises the two downloads made; both completed with zero failed videos at 1.2--1.8\,MB/s on a home connection.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Selective download of archive members}
\label{alg:download}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE archive URLs $U$, wanted video paths $W$
\FOR{each archive $u \in U$ containing wanted classes}
\STATE read the end-of-archive record and central directory of $u$ with HTTP range requests
\FOR{each member $e$ whose class folder and file name are in $W$}
\STATE fetch the local header of $e$, then its compressed bytes
\STATE decompress; \textbf{if} size or CRC-32 differ \textbf{then} record a failure
\STATE write the video atomically to its metadata path
\ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\STATE rerunning skips videos already on disk (resumable)
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Statistics}
Comparisons between models use the same test videos, and paired differences are tested with the exact McNemar test \cite{mcnemar1947}. Accuracy is top-1 accuracy over videos; macro-F1 averages the per-sign F1-scores. The gloss error rate (GER) of a sentence is the edit distance between recognised and reference gloss sequences divided by the reference length. The sign error rate (SER) of a stream counts substitutions, deletions and insertions of recognised signs relative to the reference sequence.

\section{System Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the system. The browser front end (React, TypeScript) captures the webcam, runs the MediaPipe Hand and Face Landmarkers (version 0.10.21, the same models used offline for the datasets) and sends only landmark coordinates over a WebSocket to a Python FastAPI back end. The back end segments, classifies and translates; the result is shown as Kannada text with the ISL gloss and confidence, a pending-word confirmation step with alternatives, and Kannada speech through the browser's speech synthesis. In the reverse direction, Kannada text is normalised (Unicode NFC), matched against a phrase dictionary of 187 entries by longest match, converted to an ISL gloss plan in ISL word order, and played on a three.js avatar. Words without a sign are listed as unsupported and never guessed.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Landmark Features}
Each frame is encoded as a 147-dimensional vector (schema \texttt{hands-face-v2}). For each hand, 73 values describe the wrist-relative hand shape scaled by palm size (60), the position in the image (3), the position relative to the face (3), the finger direction and palm normal (6) and a presence flag (1); one value marks face visibility. A missing hand is flagged rather than filled with zeros. During dataset extraction, if the face detector fails on the full frame, it is re-run on an upper-centre crop of the original-resolution frame; this made face-relative features reliable for signs made near the face.

A segment of variable length is resampled to $T$ evenly spaced steps and complemented by wrist velocities and path lengths, giving an input of dimension
\begin{equation}
D = 147\,T + 6\,(T-1) + 2,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $D=2{,}444$ for $T=16$ and $D=3{,}668$ for $T=24$ (current models). Each model stores its own $T$, so changing the default for new models never invalidates an existing one.

\subsection{Isolated-Sign Classifiers}
Three candidates are trained: multinomial logistic regression on standardised inputs; a bidirectional GRU \cite{cho2014gru,schuster1997bidirectional} with a per-frame linear embedding (96 units), 96 units per direction, mean and max pooling over time concatenated with global motion features, and a softmax output, trained with AdamW, class-weighted loss and early stopping; and a probability ensemble of the two. Training data is augmented with five copies per sample using temporal cropping (up to 20\%), landmark jitter, position jitter and left-right mirroring (for left-handed signers and mirrored videos). The candidate with the highest signer-grouped cross-validation macro-F1 is selected; for the current model this was the BiGRU (0.368, against 0.357 for the ensemble and 0.305 for logistic regression).

\subsection{Rejection Threshold}
Every development video receives an out-of-fold prediction, and the confidence threshold that maximises the F1-score of accepted words is selected, where a non-sign predicted as a sign counts as an error (Algorithm~\ref{alg:threshold}). Predictions below the threshold are shown as ``uncertain'' with alternatives and are never added to the transcript automatically. Fig.~\ref{fig:calibration} shows precision, recall and coverage of the current model against the threshold.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Threshold calibration}
\label{alg:threshold}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE development samples with signer groups
\STATE obtain out-of-fold class probabilities with grouped cross-validation
\FOR{$\tau = 0.05, 0.10, \ldots, 0.95$}
\STATE accept predictions with confidence $\geq \tau$
\STATE compute precision and recall of accepted words; non-signs accepted as signs count as errors
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\tau$ with the highest F1-score
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Live Segmentation}
A sign starts when hand motion exceeds a threshold and ends after a stillness period, when the hands are lowered or leave the frame for 200\,ms, or after at most 4\,s (Algorithm~\ref{alg:segment}). Training clips end with the hands moving, whereas live signers often hold the final position; the held-still frames at the end of a segment are therefore trimmed before classification. Replaying the 268 test videos through the live pipeline with the hold ending, this trimming raised the number of correctly recognised signs from 136 to 184. Repeated signs are kept: a duplicate is suppressed only within 600\,ms of the same sign.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Motion segmentation with still-tail trimming}
\label{alg:segment}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\FOR{each incoming frame $f_t$}
\STATE $m_t \leftarrow$ wrist motion between $f_{t-1}$ and $f_t$
\IF{idle \AND hands present \AND $m_t >$ start threshold}
\STATE start a segment
\ELSIF{in segment}
\STATE append $f_t$
\IF{still for the stillness period \OR hands absent for 200\,ms \OR length $>$ 4\,s}
\STATE drop the trailing still frames; classify; return to idle
\ENDIF
\ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Continuous Sentence Recognition}
A two-layer BiGRU (128 units per direction) reads every second frame of a sentence and outputs, per time step, a distribution over 168 glosses plus the CTC blank \cite{graves2006ctc}. Separately, a logistic-regression sentence classifier operates on the sentence resampled to 32 steps. For each corpus sentence $s$ with gloss sequence $g_s$, the decoder scores
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(s) = \log P_{\mathrm{CTC}}(g_s \mid X) + \lambda \log P_{\mathrm{cls}}(s \mid X),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda=1.0$ chosen on the validation round. The softmax of the scores gives a posterior; sentences with posterior below 0.5 (also chosen on validation) are shown as uncertain with the three best candidates. While a sentence is being signed, a greedy CTC decode of the frames so far is shown as tentative, never as a final result. The sentence results in Section~VII refer to this decoder.

\subsection{Meaning Layer: ISL Glosses to Kannada}
ISL word order differs from Kannada, and a gloss-by-gloss rendering is often ungrammatical. Recognised gloss sequences are therefore matched against a phrase table that maps ISL gloss sequences to an English meaning and a Kannada sentence; ambiguous sequences (for example question versus statement) list both readings, and parts outside the table are shown word by word and marked as such. Section~VII-E measures the effect of this layer.

\subsection{Avatar Clip Generation and Continuous Signing}
Each avatar clip is retargeted from a real dataset recording of the sign. Per frame, the wrist position is expressed relative to the signer's face (scaled by face width), depth is estimated from the apparent palm size, and the hand orientation (finger direction and palm normal) and the flexion of every finger joint are computed from the 21 hand landmarks; arms are assigned by image position so that mirrored videos do not cross them. The avatar solves each arm with analytic two-bone inverse kinematics. Raw landmarks are noisy and MediaPipe occasionally loses a hand for a few frames, which made the avatar's arm drop to the rest pose and jump back mid-sign. The clip builder therefore applies Algorithm~\ref{alg:avatar}. For a sentence, consecutive signs are connected by a 0.2\,s transition from the end of one sign to the start of the next, and the arms return to rest only once, after the last sign.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Avatar clip cleaning (per hand)}
\label{alg:avatar}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE per-frame hand poses $p_1, \ldots, p_n$ (or missing)
\IF{the hand is present in fewer than 15\% of frames}
\RETURN no track (detector noise)
\ENDIF
\STATE interpolate gaps of at most 0.4\,s between tracked frames
\STATE smooth position, direction, palm normal and finger flexion with a Gaussian kernel ($\sigma=1.5$ frames) within continuous runs
\STATE trim motionless frames before the first and after the last movement (keeping 0.12\,s)
\STATE resample to at most 15 keyframes per second
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Model Governance}
Every trained model is stored as a version with its metrics, its per-video test predictions and a status record. A new model becomes active only if (i) its held-out macro-F1 is at least 0.40, (ii) it is not worse than the active model by more than 0.02 on the test videos both share, and (iii) it passes a reload and live-pipeline smoke test; earlier versions remain available for rollback. Training started from the user interface for the sentence model only produces an inactive candidate, because a single training run varies by several points with the random seed (Section~VII-D).

\textit{Data collection for evaluation.} Users can record samples with the purpose ``evaluation only''; these are stored apart from training data and never trained on. Recording conditions (speed, distance, lighting and hand position) are stored with each sample, and ``guided variation'' asks for a different condition on each repetition. Sign sequences can be recorded with their declared order to measure continuous performance. A live evaluation report computes accuracy, macro-F1, rejection rate, false detections of non-signs and the sign error rate of sequences, and attributes every error to a cause: the right sign not in the top three, the right sign second or third, the right sign below the threshold, a suppressed duplicate, missing face or hands, or a wrong boundary.

\textit{Robust data handling.} Imports are resumable and skip processed videos; uploads are restricted to one sign per video with an explicit label, never guessed from file names; landmark extraction records its version with every sample; and dataset integrity is verified with checksums.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Isolated Signs}
Table~\ref{tab:iso} and Fig.~\ref{fig:results} summarise the development on the identical 268 held-out videos. Temporal modelling and face-crop extraction raised accuracy from 59.7\% to 72.4\%. The INCLUDE expansion then raised it to 79.9\%; on the 213 videos of the two unseen Amrita signers, the most demanding subset, accuracy rose from 67.1\% to 76.5\%. Paired on the same 268 videos, 34 videos became correct and 13 became wrong (exact McNemar $p=0.003$). The largest gains were for the signs that received new data, such as GREY, BLACK, BIRD, DOG, FATHER, FISH and BOY. On the full 314-video test set, the selected BiGRU reaches 81.5\% (macro-F1 0.800), against 70.7\% (0.703) for logistic regression trained on the same data.

\subsection{Meaning Layer}
To measure what sequence-level translation adds over word-by-word glossing, 31 phrases were rendered as 124 streams of held-out recordings and passed through recognition and the meaning layer. Recognition had a sign error rate of 0.301, and 75 of 124 streams (60.5\%) were recognised exactly. The Kannada output matched the reference sentence in 60.5\% of the streams through the English meaning layer, against 39.5\% for word-by-word output (chrF 0.669 against 0.605). With perfect recognition, word-by-word output would match only 61.3\%. The references are the phrase table's own draft sentences, which have not yet been reviewed by native speakers, so these figures show the relative benefit of the layer, not absolute Kannada quality. The layer cannot correct recognition errors: its exact matches coincide with the streams recognised perfectly.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\textit{Recorded video is not live use.} All accuracies above are measured on recorded dataset videos of unseen signers. Live webcam accuracy depends on camera, lighting, distance and on whether the user signs the regional variant present in the training data (Kerala for Amrita SLR, Chennai for INCLUDE). The system therefore includes an evaluation-only recording mode and a live evaluation report with error attribution; these measurements are the next step and are not yet available. The reference page shows the dataset recordings of each sign so that users can see the variant the model was trained on.

\textit{Data is the main lever.} The INCLUDE expansion improved accuracy on unseen signers more than any modelling change after the ensemble, and the gains concentrated on the signs that received data. Conversely, adding classes with recordings from few signers reduced accuracy on existing signs. With about 14--42 recordings per sign, recordings from more signers, ideally from Karnataka, matter more than larger models.

\textit{Evaluation pitfalls.} Single training runs of the sentence model vary by about nine points with the seed, INCLUDE's official split reuses signers, and variants chosen on validation folds can perform worse on test signers. The project therefore pre-registers promotion criteria, compares models on identical videos and reports seed variance; two promising candidates were rejected this way.

\textit{Vocabulary and grammar.} The sentence model is restricted to the 101 corpus sentences, so it is not open-vocabulary sign language translation. Signs performed back to back without lowering the hands can merge into one segment. Non-manual signals are only partly captured by the face-relative hand position.

\textit{Kannada and avatar.} Kannada wording received an AI-assisted check only. Avatar clips retargeted from 2-D landmarks remain approximate (no elbow tracking, estimated depth, no facial grammar), and the generic 0.2\,s transitions do not model ISL co-articulation; both the Kannada output and the clips require review by native Kannada speakers and ISL users before real use.

\textit{Ethics and privacy.} Video never leaves the user's computer, evaluation recordings are kept separate from training data, and dataset licences (CC~BY~4.0) and the privacy of the recorded signers are respected. The system is a communication aid for limited vocabularies and does not replace qualified interpreters.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
Silent Bridge demonstrates a complete, privacy-preserving ISL--Kannada pipeline on a CPU-only laptop, with signer-independent evaluation and a governance process that prevents regressions from reaching users. Additional real ISL data was the most effective single improvement, raising unseen-signer accuracy from 67.1\% to 76.5\%, and cleaning the avatar motion removed all mid-sign tracking artefacts. Future work comprises (i) live webcam evaluation sessions with several signers, (ii) recordings from Karnataka signers to cover regional variants and to support a larger recognised vocabulary, (iii) sentence-level training on larger ISL corpora such as iSign \cite{joshi2024isign}, (iv) arm and body pose for more accurate avatar motion, and (v) review of the Kannada output and avatar clips by the deaf community.
